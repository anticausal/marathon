## The five rules that make this work

1. **Easy means easy.** Almost every mile — and every cross-training minute — is at conversational effort — heart rate roughly **140–150**, capped around 150. If you can't speak full sentences, slow down or walk (or ease the gear). The exceptions are the Wednesday and Saturday club runs. Easy is what lets the long runs keep growing without wrecking you.

2. **Run/walk from the start.** Practice a ratio in training (run 4 min / walk 1 min) so it's automatic on race day. Walk breaks lower your heart rate and core temp — in Honolulu's heat they often make you *faster* overall, not slower. Every Monday long run is 4′/1′.

3. **Cutback weeks are training, not slacking.** The long run drops back in Week 22 so your body absorbs the work. Don't skip it — it's where you actually get stronger and where injuries get avoided.

4. **The long run is the priority.** Wednesday and Saturday are support runs. If you're tired, shorten or skip those before you touch Monday.

5. **The knee and the Achilles gate the ramp.** The long run steps up only after a week of clean mornings — see *The morning check*. If the knee, the Achilles or the foot talks back, repeat the distance and check with the PT or podiatrist. Capping your longest run at **13** is already baked in, and going even shorter is fine for finishing.

## Building back (Weeks 18–26)

### The Monday long run

- **Run 4′ / walk 1′ at HR 140–150** — the ratio you'll use on race day.
- **An early start means running in the dark** — headlamp and something reflective. It's also good rehearsal: Honolulu starts at 5 AM in the dark.
- **From the 9-miler on, practice your race-day food and drink** — whatever you plan to carry on Dec 13. Nothing new on race day.
- **Rest on Sunday** so you start Monday fresh.

### Wednesday and Saturday

- **The club trail runs.** 3–4 mi, usually a harder effort — the week's two harder runs, standing in for the dropped ★ sessions. Skip one, or run it easy, when the morning check isn't clean. No club that day? An easy 3–4.
- **A hard Saturday sits two days before the long run.** Sunday is full rest, and Monday's long run stays at easy effort however Saturday went.
- **Trails and hills:** climbs work the calf and Achilles harder, and descents load the knee. While either one is grumpy, walk the steep parts.

### The morning check — knee and Achilles

Adapted from the pain-monitoring approach used in Achilles rehab (Silbernagel et al., 2007; BC Physical Therapy tendinopathy toolkit, 2021):

1. **During a run:** mild discomfort is OK. Stop running and walk if the pain passes 5 out of 10, turns sharp, or changes your stride.
2. **The next morning:** back to normal — the first steps out of bed for the Achilles, the stairs for the knee. Not back to normal → easy day or rest.
3. **Week to week:** morning stiffness shouldn't creep up. If it does, hold and see the PT.

**The hold rule:** the long run steps up only if every morning that week was clean. Otherwise repeat last Monday's distance. Each repeat slides the ramp a week and takes about a mile off the peak — one repeat makes it 12. Never compress the ramp to catch up. The ramp protects the knee and the Achilles; the peak is just a number, and a run/walk finish in Honolulu doesn't depend on it.

**Tell the PT about the Achilles.** If it keeps showing up, calf strengthening (heavy calf raises) is the best-supported treatment, and it fits into the strength work you're already doing.

### Why the steps are the size they are

A 2025 study of 5,205 runners (Frandsen et al., *British Journal of Sports Medicine*) found the injury rate rose when a single run was more than 10% longer than the longest run in the previous 30 days:

| Longer than your 30-day longest by | Injury rate |
|---|---|
| Up to 10% | baseline |
| 10–30% | 1.64× |
| 30–100% | 1.52× |
| More than double | 2.28× |

Week-to-week mileage increases showed no link. The ramp has three steps over 10% — **Oct 19, Oct 26 and Nov 2**. From the Nov 9 cutback on, every step is under 10%. Those three Mondays are where the morning check matters most.

## The cross-training block (Weeks 4–10) — same effort, same time, no impact

*History — the block is done. The bike-fit notes still apply to the Tuesday spin.*

The idea, in your words: **same duration and heart-rate target, cross-training instead of running.** Each session mirrors the run it would have been:

- **Effort:** steady HR **140–150**, conversational. This is *easy* — resist the urge to hammer the erg or grind a big gear.
- **Duration:** matches the equivalent run's time on feet. Mon/Wed are short (30–50 min); Saturday is your long session, which built **50 → 70 min** over Weeks 4–7.
- **Weeks 8–10 hold, they don't build** in running terms — 70 min was the *ceiling* of the rowing ramp, not a number to keep hitting.
- **The bike arrived Aug 12** and takes over from there: Week 9's Wednesday and Saturday are rides, and **Week 10 is all bike**. The erg stays available as an anytime substitute, and mixing the two across a week is a good way to keep the hold weeks from getting stale.
- **On the bike the long session gets longer — target 90 min,** once the saddle fit is sorted (see below). Bike minutes aren't run minutes. Cycling max HR sits ~5–10 bpm below running max, so 140–150 is a slightly *harder* relative effort on the bike; but there's no impact and no eccentric loading, so extra time costs essentially nothing. The usual conversion is **long ride ≈ 1.5× the long run** it replaces, and the Saturday run you're missing is ~7–9 mi of run/walk — 85–100 min on feet. Ninety is where a long aerobic session starts holding the durability end of your fitness — fat oxidation, capillary density, and the head-space of staying in a zone for a long time — instead of just maintaining.
- **Weekday rides are 40 min,** up from the 30 that made sense on the erg. Forty minutes easy on a bike is a modest session and matches the Tue/Thu spins scheduled in the ramp weeks.
- **Spin, don't grind — this matters for the knee.** Keep cadence around **85–95 rpm with light resistance**. Low-cadence, high-resistance riding drives patellofemoral load, which is exactly the thing you're rehabbing. Check saddle height too: too low means excessive knee flexion at the top of the stroke and more load through the kneecap. Your leg should be almost straight — a slight bend — at the bottom of the pedal stroke.

### Saddle numbness — fix the fit before going long

Ride one (Aug 12) produced **numbness and tingling in the groin**. That's pressure on the **pudendal nerve**, which runs through the soft tissue between your sit bones. It's common in new riders and it's a fit problem, not something to toughen up through. Repeated compression is one of the few well-documented cycling injuries, so:

**The rule: never ride through numbness.** If it starts, stand up out of the saddle or stop the session. Numbness is the signal that blood flow and nerve conduction are already compromised.

**Padded shorts are not the fix.** They solve chafing and skin soreness. Nerve compression is about *where your weight sits*, and thick padding can even bridge the gap between the sit bones and press harder into the middle. Wear them, but fix the fit.

What actually works, in order of leverage:

1. **Raise the handlebars, and bring them closer.** Usually the biggest lever on an indoor bike and almost always adjustable. Sitting more upright rotates the pelvis back so weight lands on the sit bones instead of the perineum; low or far-forward bars tip you onto the nose of the saddle. Aim for a torso angle of ~45° or more upright — this is an easy aerobic block, there's no reason for an aggressive position.
2. **Saddle height.** Heel on the pedal at the bottom of the stroke → leg completely straight, hips level. Move to the ball of the foot and you land in the right window, ~25–35° of knee bend. Hips rocking side to side means it's too high, and that rocking grinds the perineum every stroke. This setting does double duty: too low loads the kneecap, too high causes the rocking.
3. **Saddle fore/aft.** If the saddle is slid too far forward you're sitting on the narrow nose, with no width to support the sit bones. With the cranks horizontal, the front of the forward knee should sit roughly over the pedal spindle — a starting point, not a law, but it puts you on the wide part of the saddle.
4. **Saddle tilt.** The adjustment is usually one or two bolts on the clamp *under* the saddle where the rails meet the post, not in the bike's own controls — worth a second look. Level first (phone level app), then 1–2° nose-down. More than that slides you forward onto your hands.
5. **Swap the saddle.** If the shape is wrong for your sit bone width, nothing else fully fixes it. Measure: sit on corrugated cardboard on a hard chair, lean back to load the bones, stand up, measure center-to-center between the dents, **add 20–25 mm**. Stock saddles run ~140 mm and plenty of men need 145–155. Buy one with a **cutout or relief channel**; stock saddles almost always come off standard rails.

Regardless of fit, **stand up for 20–30 seconds every 8–10 minutes** to restore blood flow, and wear the padded shorts with nothing underneath — that's how they're designed to work.

Change **one thing at a time** and test it on a short ride.

**Resolved Aug 15:** a full 60-minute ride with no groin numbness after the position changes. The long ride goes back on the bike and steps toward 90. Keep standing for 30 seconds every 8–10 minutes anyway — it's good practice on any setup. If it ever returns, the next lever is the saddle itself (step 5), and if it persists after that, mention it to the PT or your doctor rather than living with it.

### Hands and feet — the next pressure points

The Aug 15 ride produced slight numbness in the **right index finger** and **right big toe**, both clearing within 30 seconds of getting off. That's transient positional compression, not injury — but it's worth fixing rather than tolerating.

- **Index finger** is median-nerve territory: weight on the heel of the hand with the wrist bent back. (Ulnar compression, the classic "handlebar palsy," hits the ring and pinky instead.) The saddle was *not* tilted nose-down, so this isn't a forward-slide problem — it's cockpit height and hand position. **Get the bars at or above saddle height**; if they still sit below it you're carrying real weight on your hands, and on an easy indoor ride there's no reason not to be upright. Then: wrists in a neutral handshake line with the forearm rather than cocked back on the bar tops, elbows visibly bent rather than locked, light grip (you're not steering — hands can come off), and shift position every few minutes. **Padded gloves do help here** — unlike padded shorts for the groin, hand padding addresses the actual mechanism.
- **Big toe** is forefoot pressure — "hot foot." Cheapest fixes first: loosen shoes and straps a notch (feet swell during exercise); check that the **ball of the foot sits over or just behind the pedal spindle**, not the toes; and use a stiffer-soled shoe, since a flexible running sole lets the pedal edge dig into the forefoot. Worth raising with the podiatrist, especially about orthotics — ones built for running don't necessarily work on a pedal.

**Both symptoms were on the right side.** Probably just the dominant side gripping and pushing harder, and two mild symptoms is thin evidence. But asymmetric pressure at both contact points is also what unconsciously offloading one leg looks like — worth mentioning to the PT, and worth paying attention to mid-ride.

**Keep the standing breaks.** Numb at 30 minutes continuous, clean at 60 with nothing longer than a 10-minute stretch in the saddle — the intermittent unloading is likely doing more of the work than the shorts. Don't drop it once things feel fine.

Transient is fine. **Numbness that lingers for minutes or hours after the ride is not** — that means backing off and getting it looked at.
- **No ratio to hit — just steady easy effort.** Don't replicate the 4:1 run/walk on the erg or the bike. Your walk breaks are a *running* tool (they manage impact and heat and rehearse race day); cross-training has no impact and isn't race-specific, so simply hold the 140–150 zone for the whole session. The only target is time in the zone.
- **PT leg-strength: every day.** This is the whole point of the pause — the strength work is the priority, the cardio keeps the engine warm.

## Heat prep (the Honolulu-specific piece)

You'll train through a WA summer and fall — cooler and drier than race morning. You can't fully replicate Honolulu, but in the final 6–8 weeks consider a few runs slightly overdressed or on a treadmill in a warm room to teach your body to cool itself. Mostly, just go in *mentally* prepared for heat to slow you, and lean hard on the walk breaks, fluids, and ice on race day.

## Race morning quick notes

- 5:00 AM dark start — go out **easy and restrained**; the first 6 miles should feel boring.
- Run/walk from mile 1, don't wait until you're tired.
- HR ~148–158, run by effort as it heats up.
- Cool aggressively: fluids, ice, sponges at every aid station.
- Past mile 13 you're beyond your longest run — that's expected. Walk more before you have to, not after.
- No clock pressure. Everyone finishes. Your only job is to keep moving.
